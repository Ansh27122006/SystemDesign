Scalability

A system's ability to handle growing load. There are two fundamentally different ways to get there — make one machine bigger, or use more machines. Each scene below is interactive: drive the traffic yourself in the first two, then watch a full autoscaling race in the third.

A Vertical Scaling — Scaling Up

Give the existing machine more CPU, RAM, or disk. Simple to reason about, but every upgrade usually means a resize/reboot, and there's a hardware ceiling you eventually hit.

Vertical vs. Horizontal, side by side

Vertical (scale up)Horizontal (scale out)
CeilingHard limit — biggest machine money can buyVery high — keep adding machines
Downtime to scaleUsually yes (resize/reboot)No — new nodes join independently
ComplexityLow — same single systemHigher — needs a load balancer, statelessness, data partitioning
Cost curveJumps in big steps, gets disproportionately expensive at the top endGrows more linearly with each added unit
Good fitSimple apps, single-node databases, quick early-stage winsWeb/API tiers, stateless services, anything expecting unpredictable growth
In practice, most real systems use both. Databases are often scaled up (vertically) as far as reasonable before the harder work of sharding begins, while stateless application servers are scaled out (horizontally) almost by default. The autoscaling race above is a simplification, but the shapes it produces — vertical's downtime steps and horizontal's smoother capacity ramp — are exactly why cloud providers default new services to horizontal autoscaling groups.
