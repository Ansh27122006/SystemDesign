Microservices

Instead of one building with every department inside, imagine a street of independent shops — each one owns its own space, staff, and stock, and can renovate or close for the day without shutting down the whole street. That's the shift from a monolith to microservices.

Opening picture — a street of independent shops

Each "shop" — Orders, Payments, Inventory, Notifications — is its own small, independently deployable service with its own codebase, its own database, and a clearly defined "storefront" (API) that other shops talk to. Nobody reaches behind the counter into another shop's stockroom.

01 · CORE DEFINITION
What actually makes something "microservices"

Microservices architecture splits an application into small, independently deployable services, each owning a specific business capability.

TraitWhat it means in practice
Independently deployableEach service ships on its own schedule, without waiting on or rebuilding any other service.
Owns its dataEach service typically has its own database — no other service reaches into it directly.
Communicates over the networkServices call each other via APIs (HTTP/REST, gRPC) or asynchronous messages — never in-process function calls.
Organized by business capabilityBoundaries are drawn around things like "Orders" or "Payments," not technical layers like "controllers."

06 · TRADE-OFFS
Where microservices genuinely win — and where they hurt

Strengths

  • Teams deploy independently, without waiting on or blocking each other
  • Scale exactly the services under load, not the whole application
  • Different services can use different languages/databases suited to their job
  • A crash in one service doesn't necessarily take the whole system down
VS

Weaknesses

  • Distributed systems problems appear everywhere: latency, partial failure, retries
  • Testing and debugging span multiple services and machines
  • Data consistency requires real engineering effort (events, sagas, eventual consistency)
  • Operational overhead — service discovery, deployment pipelines, monitoring, per service
07 · REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES
Microservices in serious production use
🎬

Netflix — hundreds of independent services

Netflix runs over 1,000 microservices, each independently deployable, so hundreds of teams can ship changes daily without coordinating a single, shared release. They also pioneered "Chaos Monkey" to deliberately test resilience to the partial failures this architecture introduces.

SCALED WELL
🍕

Amazon's "two-pizza team" mandate

Amazon's internal mandate that every team should be small enough to feed with two pizzas, each owning its own service end-to-end via APIs only, is often credited as one of the origins of the microservices movement at scale.

ORGANIZATIONAL
🚕

Uber's microservice sprawl

Uber grew to over 2,200 microservices at one point, and later had to invest heavily in tooling just to manage the resulting operational complexity — a widely cited cautionary tale about splitting services faster than your tooling can support.

CAUTIONARY TALE
🛒

Amazon.com's checkout & catalog split

Product catalog browsing and checkout have wildly different traffic patterns and failure tolerance — splitting them lets Amazon scale browsing traffic (huge, read-heavy) completely separately from the more sensitive, transactional checkout path.

08 · COMMON CONFUSIONS
What students usually get wrong
✕
"Microservices are always the better, more modern choice."

They trade one set of problems (deploy contention, coarse scaling) for another (network latency, partial failure, operational overhead). Neither is universally "better" — it depends on team size, scale, and how independent the domains actually are.

✕
"Smaller services automatically make the system simpler."

The opposite is often true — you've moved complexity out of the code and into the network, where it's harder to see: more moving parts, more failure modes, more to monitor.

✕
"Services should be as small as possible."

Splitting too aggressively ("nanoservices") multiplies network calls for no real benefit. Good boundaries follow real business capabilities, not an arbitrary size target.

09 · IN-CLASS ACTIVITY
Good fit or bad fit for microservices?
GRADED

Should this team split into microservices?

For each scenario, decide whether microservices are a reasonable choice right now.

1. A 3-person team building a new product, with no users yet and requirements still changing weekly.
This is a bad fit, for now. Splitting services requires knowing where the boundaries should be — and that's usually unclear until the domain has stabilized. A monolith lets them change their mind cheaply.
2. A company with 200 engineers across 25 teams, where a single shared deploy pipeline has become a major bottleneck.
This is a good fit. At this scale, the organizational pain of coordinating one shared deploy across 25 teams is a concrete, specific reason microservices exist to solve.
3. A video platform where transcoding needs thousands of CPU-heavy machines, but the comments feature barely needs any compute at all.
This is a good fit. Wildly different resource needs between features is one of the clearest, most concrete reasons to split a service out on its own.
4. A small internal HR tool with 50 users total and one team maintaining it, currently working fine as a single application.
This is a bad fit. There's no scaling pain, no deploy contention, and no team-coordination problem here — splitting it into services would only add operational overhead with no real benefit.
