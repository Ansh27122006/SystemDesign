Monolith vs Microservices vs Serverless

A third option enters the picture: instead of running servers yourself — one big one (monolith) or many small ones (microservices) — serverless means writing individual functions and letting the cloud provider run them only when needed, for exactly as long as needed.

Opening picture — one building, a street of shops, or pop-up stalls

Monolith: one building, always open, every department inside, staffed and lit 24/7 whether customers are there or not.

01 · CORE DEFINITIONS
Three ways to run the same application

Same features, three very different deployment and operations models.

Monolith

One codebase, one deployment, servers you provision and keep running continuously — whether traffic is high or zero.

always-on serversyou manage capacitysimple ops model

Microservices

Many small services, each with its own servers you provision, running continuously, scaled independently per service.

always-on servers, per serviceyou manage capacity, per serviceindependent deploys

Serverless

Individual functions that the cloud provider runs on demand — spun up per request, billed per execution, scaled to zero when idle.

provider manages serverspay per invocationauto-scales to zero and back

05 · REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES
Where serverless specifically earns its keep
🖼️

Image thumbnails on upload (AWS Lambda + S3)

A classic serverless pattern: a function triggers automatically whenever a file lands in storage, resizes it, and disappears — no server sits around waiting for uploads that might happen once an hour or a thousand times a minute.

EVENT-TRIGGERED
🥤

Coca-Cola's vending machines

Coca-Cola moved vending machine payment processing to AWS Lambda — a widely cited real case study — because machine traffic is naturally bursty and unpredictable, exactly the shape serverless billing is built for.

BURSTY WORKLOAD
🎬

Netflix's media processing pipeline

Netflix uses serverless functions for parts of its video encoding pipeline, where individual processing jobs are short, numerous, and highly parallel — a good match for functions that spin up, do one job, and disappear.

PARALLEL JOBS
🧾

Seasonal, spiky businesses

Tax-filing software sees enormous traffic in the weeks before a filing deadline and near-zero the rest of the year — provisioning servers to handle peak load means paying for idle capacity most of the year, exactly what serverless avoids.

06 · COMMON CONFUSIONS
What students usually get wrong
✕
"Serverless means there are no servers."

There are absolutely still servers — you just don't manage, patch, or provision them. The cloud provider owns that responsibility instead.

✕
"Serverless is always cheaper."

For steady, high, predictable traffic, an always-on server can be significantly cheaper than paying per invocation at scale. Serverless wins specifically for spiky or unpredictable workloads.

✕
"Serverless just means AWS Lambda / functions."

Functions-as-a-Service (Lambda, Cloud Functions) are the most visible example, but "serverless" more broadly includes any managed service that scales automatically without you provisioning servers — managed databases, object storage, and more.

07 · IN-CLASS ACTIVITY
Pick the best fit
GRADED

Monolith, Microservices, or Serverless?

For each scenario, choose the architecture that fits best.

1. A 3-person team validating a brand-new product idea, aiming to ship an MVP in a few weeks.
Best fit: Monolith. Maximum speed, minimum operational complexity, and no need yet for independent scaling or deploys.
2. A function that resizes a user's profile photo whenever they upload one — maybe 5 times a minute, maybe zero for hours.
Best fit: Serverless. Wildly unpredictable, event-triggered, short-lived work is exactly the pattern serverless billing and scaling were designed for.
3. A 300-engineer company where 30 teams each need to own, deploy, and scale their part of the product independently, with sustained heavy traffic 24/7.
Best fit: Microservices. Large team count needing independent ownership, plus sustained (not spiky) heavy load, is the classic microservices sweet spot — provisioned capacity is well-utilized here, not wasted.
4. A tax-filing startup that sees 95% of its yearly traffic packed into the three weeks before the filing deadline.
Best fit: Serverless. Provisioning enough servers for peak season means paying for mostly-idle capacity the other 49 weeks of the year — auto-scaling, pay-per-use serverless avoids that entirely.
